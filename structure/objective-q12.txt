GOAL
====
Two questions about how imperfect a gravitational-wave template can be before a
signal is lost. They are the first two of five (the full set is
~/gw-minimal-match/structure/objective.txt); this job answers only these two, and is
also a measurement of what a job this size costs, so keep it small.

The setting is fixed (see SETTING below): non-spinning compact binaries with
component masses in [5, 50] Msun, one detector, Gaussian stationary noise with a
known PSD, frequency-domain templates.

1. For a true signal h and a template u at offset parameters, derive the recovered
   matched-filter SNR in terms of the match between them, exactly and then to
   leading order in the mismatch. Say when the signal is lost -- recovered SNR
   below a threshold rho* -- as a condition on the optimal SNR and the match. Keep
   rho* a symbol: how it is set is a later job.
   Use one concrete, physical imperfection as the worked example: a TaylorF2
   template recovering an IMRPhenomD signal of the same masses, and then the best
   TaylorF2 template for it (the fitting factor). Across the mass range, how large
   is that mismatch, and for what ratio of optimal SNR to rho* does it lose the
   signal?

2. Compute the match metric g_ij in chirp-time coordinates (tau_0, tau_3)
   numerically, from matches of TaylorF2 waveforms maximised over time and phase,
   and compare it with the analytic metric of Owen (1996) / Owen & Sathyaprakash
   (1999) built from the phase derivatives and the noise moments. Across the mass
   range: how well do they agree, where does the metric vary fastest, and at what
   mismatch does the quadratic approximation match ~ 1 - g_ij dtheta^i dtheta^j
   stop being good enough to place a bank with?

Out of scope for this job: detection thresholds and false-alarm rates, template
banks and lattices, Monte Carlo covering checks, volume loss. Do not start on them.


SETTING -- these choices are fixed, do not revisit them
======================================================
The source of truth is ~/gw-minimal-match/wiki/conventions.md, "Non-negotiable
choices", which also records the alternative rejected for each. In brief:

- Detector and noise: one detector, stationary Gaussian noise, one-sided PSD
  `pycbc.psd.aLIGOZeroDetHighPower`, analytic, sampled on the analysis frequency
  grid.
- Band: f_low = 20 Hz, f_high = 1024 Hz.
- Segment length: the next power of two above the longest TaylorF2 duration in
  range (the 5+5 Msun corner from 20 Hz); df = 1 / T_seg.
- Parameter space: m1, m2 in [5, 50] Msun, m1 >= m2, no spin.
- Metric coordinates: chirp times (tau_0, tau_3) referenced to f_0 = f_low = 20 Hz.
- Waveforms: TaylorF2 (3.5 PN phase) for the metric comparison; IMRPhenomD only as
  the "missing physics" signal in question 1.
- Inner product: <a|b> = 4 Re int a(f) b*(f) / S_n(f) df. Match = normalised inner
  product maximised over time and phase. Mismatch = 1 - match.


SOURCES
=======
The papers are in `papers/` in the job's work directory. Only these three are
needed here:

  gr-qc-9511032/         Owen 1996, template spacing: the metric and minimal match
                         (PDF and the arXiv LaTeX source)
  gr-qc-9808076/         Owen & Sathyaprakash 1999, the metric in chirp times
  gr-qc-0509116/         Allen et al. 2012 (FINDCHIRP), the matched-filter SNR
                         statistic

The project this job belongs to is ~/gw-minimal-match. The files that matter here:

  wiki/conventions.md                     the settled choices, with reasons
  structure/claims.yaml                   claims `mismatch-snr-loss` and
                                          `metric-vs-owen` are these two questions
  wiki/sources/owen1995_template_metric.md
  scripts/reproduce_owen1995.py,
  data/owen1995_reproduction.json         a reproduction of Owen 1996

That is prior work, not verified input. The Owen reproduction uses Owen's
historical PSD, 1PN phase and f_0 = 70 Hz, not this SETTING: take the method from
it, not the numbers. It records two misprints found by recomputing Owen 1996
(Eqs. 44-45 and 49); check them rather than inheriting them. Where something in
the project disagrees with what you find, report both and say which you believe.

You may use the standard scientific python stack and PyCBC.

WHAT YOU CANNOT GET FROM THE SOURCES
====================================
- How to sample the mass range: how many points, and where. Enough to see where
  the metric varies fastest and where the numeric and analytic metrics part; not
  more. State the choice.
- The finite-difference step for the numeric metric, and how you know the result
  is converged in it.
- Which PN order the analytic metric should be taken at, given 3.5 PN waveforms,
  and what the difference costs. Decide and show it.
- Whether these two questions are the right two. If answering them turns up
  something that makes one of them the wrong question, say so.

Do not assume the framing is correct because we wrote it.


DISCIPLINE
==========
- Environment: `~/gw-minimal-match/.venv/bin/python ...` for anything you execute.
  Do not pip install anything, into that environment or any other.
- Write the noise-weighted inner product and the time-and-phase maximisation
  yourself, and check them against `pycbc.filter.match` at several points across
  the range. Say, for every result, what was written in this job and what was
  called from a library.
- Derive symbolically where you can, and say which steps are derived and which are
  recalled.
- Every number you report must come from code in this job, or be quoted from a
  named paper with its equation, table or page. Say which, every time.
- When two sources disagree, report the disagreement with both numbers. A few per
  cent is usually a modelling choice; an order of magnitude is usually a bug. Stop
  on the second kind.
- Keep computations light: this runs on a laptop, and a grid that takes more than
  a few minutes is too fine for this job.
- Say what you did NOT check.
- No network access is required for anything here. Do not fetch data.

DELIVERABLE
===========
One self-contained HTML file, `out/report.html`, that opens from a `file://` URL
with NO network -- no CDN, no external stylesheet, no remote font, no fetch. Check
this rather than assuming it.

It is what a physicist reads: the two questions, what you computed, what came out,
and where it leaves the problem. Every figure inline, and every number tagged
[src:key] against `out/provenance.json`. Its central figures: the recovered-SNR
fraction against mismatch, with the TaylorF2-vs-IMRPhenomD mismatch across the mass
range marked on it (question 1); and the numeric against the analytic metric
components across the mass range (question 2).
